The first coins
1 rupee, half rupee, quarter rupee, 2 annas, ¼ anna, ½ pice, 1/12 anna, and the 1 anna from 1906.
People transacted at a granularity that no longer exists today.
Every one of them sat below the rupee. For the next ninety years, so did every new coin.
The War Coins
The First World War saw a rise in silver prices, so 4 annas and 8 annas were introduced, since the intrinsic value of ₹1 beat its face value.
Two more coins, ½ anna and 1 pice, were introduced during the Second World War to replace nickel, which was needed elsewhere.
The Decimals
On 1 April 1957, ₹1 became 100 paise, and six new coins were launched. In 1964 the “naya” was dropped and all six were replaced with seven coins, the only time a 3 appeared on an Indian coin.
Fourteen new denominations in eleven years, every single one below ₹1.
The High Values
India pulled itself out of a crisis, and something interesting happened.
The low-value notes became high-value coins, and that’s how we got ₹2, ₹5, ₹10 and ₹20.
For ninety years every new coin was added below the rupee. From 1992, every new one was added above it.
Now as may have become clear, not all coins survived. In fact their lifespans are quite erratic.
25 of the 30 denominations died. But ₹1, ₹2, ₹5, ₹10 and ₹20 survived.

₹1, the sole survivor, goes from being the ceiling to the floor, by value and by size.
So how much further can a one-rupee coin shrink?
Maybe not much. India’s coins are designed so each one steps up in size as its value goes up, so a hand can tell them apart. That makes ₹1, at 20 mm, the anchor everything else is measured against.
The rupee is the unit this whole currency is named for.
It began as the largest coin India ever made.
It is now the smallest coin in circulation.
Not by decision, but because inflation pushes the metal down, while the law refuses to let the floor rise.

For 126 years, what set the size of a one-rupee coin was the price of metal.
Now what sets it is whether you can feel the difference.
So how much did your “change” change?
Well, one could say it went for a 180 degree toss!
Methods and notes
This piece counts coins minted for circulation. Commemorative issues are excluded throughout.
Coin specifications
Diameter, weight, alloy and years of issue for 112 versions of 30 denominations, 1900 to 2026. Specifications for coins currently in issue are published by the India Government Mint (SPMCIL); earlier coins are compiled from Numista, whose entries carry Krause catalogue numbers. Every circle in the beeswarm, every tile in the size mosaic and every figure on the one rupee screens is drawn from this set at true relative scale. Four of the 112 have no recorded diameter, so the mosaic draws 108.
Births and deaths
First and last year of minting for each denomination, from the same set. Where the mint and the catalogue disagree, the mint’s figure is used.
Coins in circulation
Reserve Bank of India, Handbook of Statistics on the Indian Economy 2025-26, Table 151: Notes and Coins in Circulation, released 31 July 2026. Shares are each denomination’s value as a proportion of all coins in circulation, small coins included and one rupee notes excluded. The Reserve Bank does not publish a one rupee coin figure for 1991-92 or 1992-93; the river chart interpolates across those two years.
Production
Security Printing and Minting Corporation of India, 17th Annual Report 2021-22. Production is published as a single consolidated figure and is never split by denomination. Per person figures divide it by mid year population from the United Nations World Population Prospects, 2024 revision.
Legal tender limits
The Coinage Act, 2011 (Act 11 of 2011), Section 6.
